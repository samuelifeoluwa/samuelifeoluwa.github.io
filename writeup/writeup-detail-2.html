Anatomy of a Banco do Brasil Tax-Refund Phishing Email

Phishing Analysis Email Forensics SPF / DKIM / DMARC Threat Intel CyberChef Blue Team

Summary & Verdict

This is a Portuguese-language phishing email impersonating Banco do Brasil (BB), one of Brazil's largest banks. It is built around an IRPF (Imposto de Renda Pessoa Física — Brazilian personal income tax) refund lure, a seasonal theme attackers lean on heavily around tax time. Despite passing every email-authentication check, it is phishing with high confidence.

The verdict rests on three findings, developed in detail below: the message was sent from a throwaway Gmail account impersonating a national bank — a domain-versus-brand mismatch; its true origin and relay infrastructure were already reported for abuse; and its embedded link pointed to an Azure-hosted credential-harvesting page that had since been taken down. No part of the analysis required executing the payload.

1. The Lure

The email presents a polished Banco do Brasil notice claiming that a refund of R$ 4.593,21 is available and will be credited to the recipient's account within 24 hours, behind a single prominent CLIQUE AQUI ("click here") button. It pulls the three classic social-engineering levers at once: money (an unclaimed refund), authority (a national bank), and urgency (a 24-hour window). It even reproduces Banco do Brasil's genuine registration number, CNPJ 00.000.000/0001-91, as a legitimacy prop — a real, publicly-available detail stitched onto a fake notice to make it harder to dismiss.

Translated to English, the notice reads:

Notification – BANCO DO BRASIL S.A
CNPJ: 00.000.000/0001-91

You have a refund of available funds — receive it now in your account.
Available amount: R$ 4,593.21
[ CLICK HERE ]

After the request, the amount will be automatically credited to your account within 24 hours.
📸 The phishing email as the victim sees it — a spoofed Banco do Brasil refund notice.

2. Header & Authentication Analysis

Email headers are written in order, so the earliest handling server sits at the bottom of the Received chain and each later hop is prepended above it. Reading from the bottom up therefore reconstructs the message's real path — and that path did not begin at any bank. The earliest hop shows the message was submitted from a host at 20.97.213.223 into smtp.gmail.com using ESMTPSA. The trailing "A" denotes an authenticated submission: a valid Gmail login was used to send it, which means the account is either attacker-registered or compromised — not spoofed.

From there the message relayed through Google's infrastructure (mail-qt1-f178.google.com, 209.85.160.178) and was received by Microsoft's Office 365 protection stack. SPF, DKIM and DMARC all returned pass — and that is exactly the trap.

The decisive indicator is the domain-versus-brand mismatch. Those authentication checks validate the sending domain — gmail.com — not the "Banco do Brasil" brand the victim sees. Because the mail genuinely was sent through Gmail from a real Gmail account, SPF/DKIM/DMARC correctly pass; they are doing their job. The brand only ever appears in the display name and the attachment image, neither of which any protocol authenticates. A legitimate Banco do Brasil message would arrive from a bancodobrasil.com.br domain, never a personal Gmail address.

Key takeaway: "Did authentication pass?" is the wrong question. The right one is "does the authenticated domain match the sender it claims to be?" Passing SPF/DKIM/DMARC confirms who sent the mail, not that the mail is safe.

3. Sender Infrastructure & IP Reputation

Both relevant hops were enriched against reputation sources. The true origin, 20.97.213.223, belongs to Microsoft Azure (Boydton, Virginia) and had already been reported on AbuseIPDB for hacking and brute-force activity — consistent with a disposable cloud VM spun up to send mail and discarded. The Google relay, 209.85.160.178, carried 168 abuse reports for email spam, which is expected for a shared outbound mail server but still worth noting.

One pattern ties the infrastructure together: the attacker is Azure-centric on both ends. The sending host is an Azure VM, and — as the link analysis shows — the phishing page itself is hosted on Azure static storage. Reusing one cloud provider for both sending and hosting is cheap and fast to stand up, which is precisely why it suits low-effort, high-volume campaigns.

4. Payload & Encoding Analysis

The From and Subject headers were not plain text — they were wrapped as RFC 2047 encoded-words (the =?utf-8?B?…?= form), a legitimate mechanism for carrying non-ASCII characters in headers that also conveniently hides the content from a casual glance. Base64-decoding them exposed the impersonation and the lure in clear text: the display name "[BB] - Seu saldo foi liberado - Código: 11084339647130851921" and the subject "Liberação de IRPF" (Release of Income-Tax Refund).

The attachment's Content-Type claimed to be a PDF — but Content-Type is attacker-controlled metadata and can say anything. Rather than trust it, I decoded the Base64 body and verified the file by its signature (magic bytes): the decoded stream begins with %PDF, which confirms a genuine PDF regardless of what the headers claim. CyberChef then rendered it, reproducing the branded refund notice.

Two deliberate evasion choices stand out. First, the entire notice is text rendered as an image — there is no selectable text for a content filter to key on (words like restituição or clique aqui are invisible to a scanner). Second, it is delivered inside a PDF attachment, which smuggles the clickable link past gateways that inspect the email body but do not follow links buried in attachments. Neither technique is advanced, but together they meaningfully raise the odds of reaching an inbox.

5. Link Analysis

Rather than clicking the link, I extracted it from the decoded content and defanged it in CyberChef — rewriting https to hxxps and . to [.] so the URL can be shared and documented without anyone (or any tool) accidentally resolving it:

hxxps://okokoaksoa[.]z13[.]web[.]core[.]windows[.]net/

The host is an Azure Blob static-website endpoint (*.web.core.windows.net). This is a recurring abuse pattern: attackers host the credential-harvesting page on a trusted cloud provider's domain so it inherits a Microsoft-owned hostname and a valid TLS certificate, which slips past naive "is it HTTPS / is the domain reputable" checks. Submitting the URL to urlscan.io and Hybrid Analysis returned "domain does not exist", meaning the page had already been taken down or removed by the operator by the time of analysis — common for short-lived phishing infrastructure.

7. Conclusion & Recommendations

Verdict: phishing, high confidence. Three defensive takeaways:

  • Judge the authenticated sending domain against the brand being claimed. "Did authentication pass?" is the wrong question; "does the authenticated domain match the sender it claims to be?" is the right one.
  • Treat links to newly-seen cloud-hosted domains such as *.web.core.windows.net with suspicion — legitimate banks do not host customer portals on generic cloud-storage endpoints.
  • Reinforce user awareness around unsolicited refund and payout lures, which pair a financial incentive with artificial urgency — and verify the attachment type by signature, never by its stated Content-Type.

A closing analyst's note: this sample is convincing but not technically sophisticated — a throwaway Gmail account, a public CNPJ, text baked into an image, and a disposable Azure VM. The craft is in the social engineering, not the tradecraft. Keeping those two apart is what stops an analyst from over-attributing a commodity phish — and knowing the difference is half the job.
