Exposed PII on Public Data-Broker Sites

OSINT Ethics Privacy Responsible Disclosure

Overview

A short reflection on a privacy issue I came across, what I did about it, where my reasoning went wrong, and the principle I would apply next time. The point of this piece is the judgement call, not the data — so no site is named and no real personal information is reproduced.

What I found

While browsing, I found a public data-broker site that exposes individuals' full names, known addresses, phone numbers, relatives, and criminal/traffic records. To test its reach, I searched several names; coverage appeared limited to US residents. Using only free, unauthenticated searches — no payment or subscription — I retrieved full names, addresses, phone numbers, and records for several of my own relatives and a couple of friends. This was only partial data; the full profiles sit behind a paywall.

Why I thought it was fine

My reasoning drew on passive reconnaissance in ethical hacking: you gather information without interacting with the target, and if you find a risk, you may report it to the owner. Reporting is legal. Exploiting the finding without explicit permission is not — and disclosing it afterward does not undo that.

Where my reasoning broke

My error was in the model, not the method. Responsible disclosure fits a system owner with a fixable flaw. An individual has no flaw to patch and never opted into a disclosure relationship, so the same approach reads as intrusion, not help. Consent, not legality, was the missing piece.

The corrected principle

Obtain consent before performing OSINT on an individual. A simple "Can I show you what's publicly exposed about you?" reframes it from intrusion to assistance.

Takeaways

  • Ask first — consent before you look.
  • Disclose privately — to help the affected person only.
  • Never broadcast — don't share the details onward.
